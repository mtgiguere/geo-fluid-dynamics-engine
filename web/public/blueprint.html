Geo-Fluid Dynamics Engine · The Blueprint

Do ideas travel across the ground
the way forests do?

One person, spare evenings, a few months, and a question that wouldn't go away. This is the whole arc: the brainstorm, the modules we imagined, the data we gathered and the machinery we built, what the data actually said when we tested our ideas against it, the plan we made for November 3, 2026, and the moment we locked it in. Written for a curious reader; the technical detail folds open where it matters.

1The brainstorm

Ideas are shared with the people we actually talk to.

At church, at school pickup, at little league, on the porch. If that's how ideas move, then they should move across a map the way a forest spreads: from where they are to where they aren't, neighbor to neighbor, over years. A documentary about far-right support in Europe made the question concrete. The German districts voting for the far right today are, to a striking degree, the districts that voted for the Nazi party in the 1930s. Dormant for three generations, then reactivated. Grandparents' ideas, still etched into the ground.

Politics turned out to be the perfect laboratory for the question, not because politics is the point, but because it is the only human idea with free, certified, county-by-county measurements, repeated every two years for a hundred and fifty years. If ideas travel across the ground, votes are where the footprints would show.

Two worries from the start, both honest: people move, and people have the internet. Both got answered by the data later. Hold them.

A geographer named Torsten Hägerstrand asked the same question in 1952 about farm innovations in Sweden. We are asking it about ideas, with better data and worse odds.

For the technically minded

Hägerstrand's spatial diffusion model (hearth, contagious expansion, hierarchical leapfrogging) is the classic frame. The persistence literature is the second anchor: Cantoni, Hagemeister and Westcott (2019) on Nazi-era vote share predicting AfD support by municipality, with activation by a later shock, and Voigtländer and Voth (2012) on medieval pogroms predicting 1920s antisemitism. The engine's data spine: county presidential returns 1868–2024 (Algara–Sharif joined to MIT), ACS demographics, Census county geometry, and certified state ballot-measure canvasses.

2The modules we imagined

Five instruments for one question.

The original plan broke the question into five pieces, from "where is an idea going" to "when do the rules of the game break." Each got a status badge from the data, not from us.

1 · Wave Predictor

Where change starts and where it spreads next: neighbor-to-neighbor diffusion.

Built the measuring tools (neighbor weights, clustering tests, a spatial regression). The "wave" itself turned out to be a flock turning together, not a front moving.

core built · wave reframed

2 · Gravity Engine

Which places lead and which follow: the influence hubs.

Two serious attempts to find leaders and followers were falsified on real data. The replacement, issue resistance as a stable county trait, is the engine's best finding.

hubs falsified · trait found

3 · Friction & Dissonance

Where a place's vote on an issue diverges from its party label.

Live on the map for Kansas, Kentucky and Ohio abortion measures and Missouri's whole record. The False Bastions.

live

4 · Chaos & Anomaly Sensor

When a variable that used to be noise becomes the signal, like education in 2016.

The seed exists: each county's surprise against its own trend. The live monitor is not built.

seed

5 · Phase Transition

When a losing side stops accepting the result: structural early warning.

Not built. One order parameter is registered for November: whether ballot measures are becoming party-line votes.

not started · one gauge registered
3Gathering, building, testing

Free data, tested code, and a rule: no claim without a failing test first.

The data

County presidential returns for every election since 1868, joined across two sources and cross-checked where they overlap. Demographics for every county from the Census. County shapes. And the expensive part: certified ballot-measure results, county by county, from each state's election office, each in its own format. Missouri's came as PDF canvasses and turned out to be the cleanest data any state gave us: 21 statewide measures since 2018 on the same 116 jurisdictions.

The machinery

Which counties touch which. A test for whether a pattern on the map is clustered or random. A regression that lets neighbors influence each other, with an honesty check that the influence isn't just neighbors looking alike. A way to measure how far a county's issue vote runs ahead of its party. A county's record read as turnout ground, persuasion ground, or hard ground. A plan generator that prices a county in votes.

The discipline

Every piece of machinery was written test-first: a failing test, then the smallest code that passes it. Every loader of real data had to reproduce its state's certified totals to the vote before anything downstream could use it. Numerical code is verified without random seeds, on constructions where the right answer is known exactly. And every hypothesis was set up to be falsified: we decided in advance what result would mean we were wrong.

For the technically minded

Queen adjacency with row-standardised weights; global and local Moran's I with permutation significance; a maximum-likelihood spatial-lag model with a Durbin control; an attribute-similarity k-nearest-neighbour network as the rival to geography; partisanship-controlled issue residuals (OLS); record-based buckets with consistency and a midterm penalty; a side-agnostic playbook generator; a scorer with relevelling. 103 tests, 98% branch coverage, mutation testing as the watchdog for hollow tests. One state's loader is days of work; the certified-total check is its lie detector.

4What the data said

Eight results, including the two that said no.

In the order the argument built. Every chart is the research notebook's own output; the page cannot show a figure the analysis didn't produce.

FINDING 1

We could not find the first domino. Twice.

The founding hope was a wave predictor: find where change starts, forecast where it spreads. The first attempt looked for counties that moved before their neighbors. Every county moves at once: swing is so clustered that leaders can't be told from followers. The second attempt, on 150 years of returns, looked for timing. The signal exists but is weak, spatially incoherent, and its extremes are tiny idiosyncratic counties, not hubs.

So the flock turns together. This is a result, not a failure, and it decided everything after it: the formation is what's predictable; the gust that turns it is not.

FINDING 2

Party is a label. Disposition is the signal.

Over the long span the South and the Northeast swapped party labels while their underlying dispositions barely moved. So the thing to track is not the party vote but the disposition, and the cleanest place to see disposition stripped of party and personality is a direct vote on an issue: a ballot measure.

FINDING 3

False Bastions, on both axes.

Places whose issue vote runs ahead of their partisan lean: red on the surface, movable underneath. In Ohio, where the same 88 counties voted on abortion and cannabis the same day, a county that broke from its party on one broke on the other.

r = 0.62

The trait is a property of the place, not of one issue. And on the economic axis the pattern inverts the "What's the matter with Kansas" story: across ten minimum-wage measures, the gap between the issue vote and the party vote is largest where the state is reddest.

FINDING 4

Geography beats similarity. Education is the runner-up.

The first worry from the brainstorm, the internet. Does change travel across borders or between places that resemble each other, college town to college town? We built a network of look-alike counties and raced it against plain adjacency.

0.62 vs 0.28

Geography wins decisively. "Ideas hop metro to metro" is not the story. Among the ways counties can resemble each other, education matters most and survives the controls. The second worry, that people move, turns out to reinforce the pattern: movers sort themselves toward places like the ones they left.

FINDING 5

Realignments flicker. They don't ratchet.

Measured election by election, the education cleavage was invisible through 2012, lit up in 2016 and 2020, and vanished in 2024, while geography stayed the dominant organizer throughout. The "diploma divide" defined two elections' swings, not a new permanent map. Anyone who called it permanent in 2020 was wrong by 2024.

FINDING 6

A county has a personality, and it repeats.

Missouri's 21 certified measures on the same 116 jurisdictions let us ask whether the Ohio result was a fluke. Abortion resistance and minimum-wage resistance on the same 2024 ballot correlate at 0.52, a social measure and an economic one. Minimum wage 2018 predicts minimum wage 2024 across six years. And a sharp caveat appeared: the trait coheres within an electorate type and barely across them. Who shows up is part of what a place is.

FINDING 7

Partisanship is the bar. The same-issue residual clears it.

Before predicting anything we pretended each past measure hadn't happened and predicted it from the others. Partisanship alone misses the typical county by 3.69 points. The fancier pooled "ideal point" we had designed scored 3.80 and lost. Partisanship plus half of a true sibling measure's residual scored 3.39. The method only helps when the history genuinely rhymes, so that's the only place we use it.

FINDING 8

Deep time: the Populist reservoir discharged into the New Deal.

The brainstorm's hunch, tested on our own ground. The 1892 Populist vote did not become 1912 Progressivism; the places that carried the idea sat out a generation. But it did predict which counties broke from their forty-year trend in 1932, when the Depression and a willing party arrived. Structure waited forty years for a catalyst. The German persistence story, in Kansas and Nebraska.

r = +0.38
5The plan for November

If the method is real, it should work on a vote that hasn't happened.

So we chose the hardest honest test available: Missouri's November 3, 2026 ballot. Five statewide measures, including the repeal of the 2024 abortion amendment and a referendum on the legislature's new congressional map, predicted for every one of the 116 jurisdictions, plus statewide calls for Nevada and Massachusetts. Each statewide number is a range, because the national mood is the one thing the data says we cannot predict; each county is a point, because the formation holds.

The plan also contains the prescriptive half: for each measure and for each side, a campaign plan priced in votes, sealed as fingerprints and revealed after the scorecard, so nobody can say the advice was written after the fact.

Read the November receipt →

The yardstick was chosen before the data exists: our county errors against partisanship alone, a calibration band, and the statewide calls graded as the judgement they are.

For the technically minded

Model: logit-linear on 2024 two-party presidential share plus 0.5 × the same-issue analog's partisanship residual, levelled to a stated statewide bracket with 2018-midterm turnout weights. Pre-registered test: one-sided paired Wilcoxon on absolute county errors over 580 rows, both model and baseline relevelled to the certified statewide share. Diagnostics registered on the engine itself: Moran's I of the Amendment 3 errors (does the spatial layer hold information the model left out?) and the correlation of errors with the 2026/2018 turnout ratio (did composition drive the misses?). One Module 5 gauge: whether the 2026 partisan slope is at least as steep as 2024's.

6Locking it in

Registered 2026-10-14. Never edited again.

On 2026-10-14, every prediction file was copied to a registered name and committed to the public repository under the tag registration-2026-11-03. A test now re-hashes every registered file on every run of the test suite; any commit, on any branch, that changes a byte of them fails. The raw inputs are fingerprinted alongside, so anyone can confirm in December that the predictions came from exactly these certified files. The ten sealed playbooks exist only as digests until the scorecard is public.

A later revision, if any, is a new dated file. The original still gets scored.

Then the honest part of the method, the part the data taught us: wait. The flock turns on November 3. A provisional scorecard follows within a day from the election-night counts; the certified one when the Board of State Canvassers signs, usually early December. Win or lose, both go here.

7So what

Why an organization would buy this, and what it gets.

The problem it solves

Campaigns for ballot measures spend money by county and by media market, but the tools they buy score individuals by party. On a ballot question the whole contest is the voters who split from their party, and a partisan score cannot see them by construction. So the budget gets split by instinct, the volunteers go where it's comfortable, and nobody is ever told afterward whether the map was right.

What you get

A county receipt for every jurisdiction: its full record on every certified measure, next to the state's, checkable line by line. A plan priced in votes: where a point of persuasion buys the most, where showing up is the whole job, where to spend nothing, and how the money divides, with your own cost per vote plugged in. The other side's best plan from the same record, so you know where to expect them. A statewide call with a range and a straight answer to whether your money changes the odds or only the margin. And a grade: predictions written down before the vote and scored after it, against the obvious alternative.

What it costs you

Your state's certified county results file, which your election office already publishes, and one careful human decision about which side of the measure is which. The loader for a new state is days of work with a built-in lie detector: it must reproduce the state's certified totals before anything is built on it. No voter file, no polling, no license to a data broker.

What it is not

Not a replacement for your voter file; that still tells the canvasser who to knock. Not a promise of a lift. Nobody has yet followed one of these plans in a campaign, so nobody, including us, knows how many points doing so produces. In December we can say whether we could see the ground. We cannot say whether acting on it moves votes until someone acts on it.

Which is the offer

The first organization to run the plan becomes the measurement: the plan in half of a matched set of counties, nothing different in the other half, the effort logged by county, and the difference read in the certified results. That campaign learns what its effort bought, in votes, by county, for the first time. Every campaign after it gets that number with a receipt. We price the ground; the first client tells us all what the plow is worth.

Every consultant has a map. This one is priced in votes, shows you the other side's map too, and has been graded in public.

To see the idea at the smallest scale, one volunteer with one free Saturday and a tank of gas, the Kansas example is the place to start.
