Geo-Fluid Dynamics Engine · The November Receipt

We wrote down Missouri's results
before Missouri voted.

Seven ballot questions on November 3, 2026. For the five in Missouri, a prediction for every one of the state's 116 counties and cities. All of it committed to a public record before the election, graded in public after it, win or lose. This page is the plain-language version of that promise: what we did, why the geography matters, what we expect, and how we'll know if we were wrong.

Draft of 2026-10-13. The registration commit freezes these exact numbers by October 15, 2026; after that they are never edited, and the scorecard runs against them.
1What we did

Twenty-one past votes, one county personality.

Since 2018 Missouri has voted on 21 statewide ballot questions: abortion, wages, marijuana, gambling, taxes, redistricting, Medicaid. Every one is reported by the Secretary of State for the same 116 places, and every one of those files was checked against the certified statewide totals before we used a single number.

From that record, two things explain almost every county's vote on almost any question. First, partisanship: how the county voted for president. It alone gets you within about four points. Second, a personality: some counties run ahead of their politics on certain issues, some run behind, and they keep doing it, election after election.

We only trust what has been tested. So before predicting anything, we pretended each past vote hadn't happened yet, predicted it from the others, and kept score.

Partisanship alone misses the typical county by 3.69 points. Our method misses it by 3.39. Everything on this page rests on that gap.

For the technically minded

Leave-one-out backtest on 13 general-election measures, statewide level given, scored as county RMSE in percentage points. Partisanship only (logit-linear on the era's presidential two-party share): 3.69. A pooled ideal-point position across the other measures: 3.8, i.e. it lost to partisanship. Partisanship plus half of the same-issue analog's partisanship residual: 3.39 against 3.76 on the same subset. Shrinkage 0.5 chosen by the table. Era-matched baselines so realignment drift can't leak in.

2Where geo-fluid dynamics comes in

Ideas move like a flock, not like dominoes.

We began with a hunch that political change spreads county to county, the way a stone's ripple crosses a pond: find the first domino and you can forecast the wave. We tested that twice on 150 years of county returns, and twice the data said no. When Missouri moves, nearly every county moves in the same election. There is no leader bird. A flock of starlings turns all at once.

What does hold is the formation. Neighboring counties vote together far more than counties that merely resemble each other, and each county keeps its place in the flock from one election to the next. That is why a county's record is such a strong predictor: the formation is stable, and the few birds flying out of formation, the counties running ahead of their politics on an issue, keep flying there.

For this ballot that means three things. The flock will turn together, so we give the statewide result as a range, not a number. The formation will hold, so each county's prediction is a point. And the counties out of formation are exactly where the November map will look different from the party map.

The honest part: this forecast uses the formation, not the flock's physics. We registered a test to find out whether that was a mistake.

For the technically minded

Spatial autocorrelation of residual presidential swing along geographic (queen) adjacency: Moran's I 0.62, versus 0.28 for the best attribute-similarity network; the spatial-lag coefficient survives a Durbin control for neighbors' demographics. Two attempts at leader-follower (contemporaneous conformity; lead-lag timing on the 1868–2024 spine) were falsified. The registered model is partisanship plus a same-issue residual and uses no spatial weights; the registered diagnostic is Moran's I of the relevelled Amendment 3 county errors (expected positive and significant), which would show the spatial layer holds information the model left on the table.

Why this is different

Campaigns already have maps. Voter-file vendors score individuals by party; consultants turn the scores into walk lists; pollsters supply the statewide number. That stack is built for candidates, it is expensive, and nobody grades it afterward. This is the layer above it:

  • It models the measure, not the party. On a ballot question the whole game is the voters who split from their party. A partisan score cannot see them by construction; twenty-one certified measure votes in the same counties can.
  • It prices the ground; the voter file lists the people. Budgets are spent on counties and media markets, not individuals. This answers the treasurer's question in votes per point; the walk list answers the canvasser's.
  • It shows you the other side's best plan. The same record yields your opponent's optimal allocation. Know where to expect them.
  • Turnout versus persuasion is measured, not guessed. The midterm penalty says, county by county, how many of your supporters skip this kind of election.
  • It has been graded. Every number here is committed before the vote and scored in public after it, against the obvious alternative. Ask anyone else for their last scorecard.
  • It is cheap and fast. Public data, tested code, a new state in days.

What it is not: a replacement for the voter file, or a promise of a lift. How many points a plan actually produces is measurable only by running it. The first campaign that follows this plan in half of a matched set of counties, and not the other half, becomes that measurement, and every campaign after it gets the answer with a receipt.

4What we predict, and why

Five Missouri questions, county by county.

Each card gives the statewide call as "N of every 100 votes" for the side we track, the range we'd stop being surprised in, and the reasoning in plain words. The side we track is a bookkeeping convention, not an endorsement: the same record produces the plan for either side.

Amendment 3

Repeal the 2024 reproductive-freedom amendment and ban most abortions?

55 of every 100 votes for NO (keep the 2024 rights)
Range we'd stop being surprised in: 52 to 59. Counties we expect that side to win: 11 of 116. Kansas City: 82.
Chance the NO side is ahead on election night, as things stand: 99%. One statewide point its way (about 23,687 net votes) moves that to over 99%.

In 2024, 51.6% of Missourians voted to protect abortion rights on a presidential turnout. A near-total ban asks a harsher question than a viability right did: in Kansas, Kentucky and Ohio the ban side ran 8 to 15 points behind its state's partisan lean. The crowd that votes in a midterm is smaller and a little older, but August's 80-to-20 rejection of the legislature's initiative restriction says the anti-legislature mood is real. Our middle call is 55 of every 100 for NO; the low end is the 2024 coalition merely holding.

Learned from: 2024 Amendment 3, 51.6% yes

6How we'll grade ourselves

The yardstick was chosen before the data exists.

The county pattern. After the certified results arrive, every county's error is compared with the error of partisanship alone, both lined up to the actual statewide result so the comparison is about where, not how much. If we aren't better than the party map, we'll say so.

The ranges. About nine counties in ten should land inside their range. Far fewer means we were overconfident; far more means we padded.

The statewide calls. Judgement, scored as judgement: did the real number land inside the range, and how far from the middle.

Twice. A provisional scorecard from election-night counts within a day, and a certified scorecard when the Board of State Canvassers signs, usually early December.

The sealed envelope. For every measure we also wrote a campaign plan for each side, priced in votes. In a public repository a committed file is a published file, so the plans are sealed as fingerprints: the digests below are committed now, the files are revealed after the scorecard, and anyone can verify they match.

Registered propositions and the statistical test
  • scoring_rule_pattern_skill — PRE-REGISTERED TEST. Pattern skill is judged on all Missouri county x measure rows (5 x 116 = 580) after BOTH the registered prediction and the partisanship-only baseline are relevelled to the certified statewide share (scoring.relevel). H1: the registered model's absolute county error is smaller than the baseline's. Our call: model beats baseline (skill > 0)
  • diag_spatial_residuals — DIAGNOSTIC (the geo in geo-fluid). The registered model uses no spatial machinery. If neighbouring counties share local factors the model does not see, its county errors will cluster in space: Moran's I of the relevelled Amendment 3 errors (queen adjacency, row-standardised W, spatial.moran) will be positive and significant at 0.05 by permutation. Our call: I > 0, significant
  • diag_turnout_composition — DIAGNOSTIC (who shows up). Turnout composition is assumed, not modelled. If the misses are the counties whose electorate changed most, the county error will correlate with the change in turnout: Pearson r between the relevelled Amendment 3 county error and (2026 votes cast / 2018 votes cast) across the 116 jurisdictions. Our call: |r| < 0.3 (composition not the dominant miss)
  • mo_2026_amdt3_coupling — The 2026 Amendment 3 county vote is at least as party-coupled as the 2024 abortion vote: OLS slope of logit(NO share) on logit(2024 Dem two-party share) >= the 2024 slope. Our call: HOLD-or-STEEPEN (slope_2026 >= slope_2024 - 0.05)

7What we are not claiming

A tool, not an oracle. It says where a conversation is worth having; the conversation is still yours.

We cannot predict the national mood that turns the whole flock; that's why the statewide numbers are ranges. We cannot name the county where a movement starts; we tried, and the data refused. We do not know who votes, only how counties vote, and if a brand-new electorate shows up the misses will be in the counties where turnout changed most. We registered a test for that too. One state, one cycle: evidence, not proof. The point of writing it down first is to find out.

Every number here is computed by tested code from certified public records: six Missouri canvasses, the Election Night Results portal, and county presidential returns. The prediction files, the methods notebook, the backtest and the sealed manifest are in the public repository, and the raw inputs are fingerprinted alongside them.
